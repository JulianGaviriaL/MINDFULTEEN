Supplementary methods
FMRI data acquisition
Neuroimaging data were collected using a 3T Magnetom TIM Trio scanner (Siemens, Germany) at the Brain and Behavior Laboratory  of the University of Geneva (Switzerland).  We used a 32-channel head-coil. Dynamic functional connectivity of the resting state was computed from Blood Oxygenation Level Dependent (BOLD) contrast from a T2*-weighted echo-planar sequence (EPI). 2160 functional volumes (1080 per visit) of 60 axial slices each (TR/TE/flip angle = 2100ms/25ms/60°, FOV=212 mm, resolution=106×106, isotropic voxels of 2 mm3, distance factor 10%) were acquired. Furthermore, we collected a high-resolution T1-weighted anatomical image [TR/TI/TE/flip angle=1900ms/900ms/2.27ms/9°, FOV=256mm, resolution=256×256, slice thickness=1mm3, 192 sagittal slices, phase encoding direction= Anterior-Posterior (AP). Our multiband sequence included 4 dummy scans (~ 5s) at the beginning of the fMRI scanning.

Preprocessing for co-activation pattern (CAP) analysis 
Following Bolton et al.,(Bolton et al., 2020) standard image preprocessing procedures were applied using the DPABI toolbox(Yan, Wang and Lu, 2021). Time-series from each voxel in the white matter and cerebrospinal fluid, plus the six affine motion parameters from realignment (also including their respective first-order derivatives), were used as nuisance variables to be regressed out from the data. No global signal regression (GSR) was carried out given the absence of agreement in the field in this regard(Murphy and Fox, 2017). Although some studies support this approach(Zhang et al., 2020; Li et al., 2021), others suggest that GSR does not affect the CAP extraction procedure(Liu and Duyn, 2013) nor measures of anticorrelation among intrinsic functional brain networks(Huang et al., 2020).  Furthermore, the data were band-pass filtered between 0.01 and 0.10 Hz.  All image volumes with frame-wise displacement above 0.5 mm were discarded, as well as subjects with more than 30% of scrubbed frames(Power et al., 2014). 

FMRI Task
The task was presented in two sessions, with 6 runs in each session. The order of the runs was pseudorandomized into 6 lists. The experimental runs consisted of an information screen (5sec) signaling the session will be evaluated; followed by a series of 5 time-limited challenging mental calculations, followed by positive or negative feedback with a social ranking (high for positive, low for negative) last lasted for 8 sec. After the feedback, participants had a 90 sec recovery period (i.e., resting state), where an information screen for 5 sec was presented which instructed the participants to close eyes and rest, then the screen turned black for 85 sec. Participants were signaled the end of the rest period by 2 flashes of light and a sound. There were 8 experimental runs, and 4 control runs where the information screen signaled the session will not be evaluated, the calculations were simple, and the feedback screen had a neutral feedback with no ranking. The recovery period information screen and time was the same for all runs.  The whole task lasted around 25 minutes. The participants’ eye movements were observed during the experiment to make sure they closed their eyes during the recovery period.
Linear mixed modelling
We performed a 2 × 2 x 3 LMM-based factorial analyses denoted by the following R-based formula syntax:
 entries ? group * measurement * condition +(1 |subject ),
where the ?xed factor “group” comprised two levels corresponding to assigned cohorts in the randomization (i.e., “treatment” and “control”). The second fixed factor “measurement” included the measurements recorded in relation to the MBI mindfulness intervention (i.e., “pre” and “post”). The fixed factor “condition” comprised the type of feedback in the MIST task preceding the resting periods (i.e., “neutral”, “negative”, and “positive”). Individual participants (i.e. “subject”) were modeled as a random factor, and the “entries” were the dependent variable, computed for each CAP. Preliminary inspections of the models examined the compliance with the assumptions for linear regressions in terms of influential observations (i.e., outliers), homogeneity of variance, normality of residual, and collinearity. Additionally, posterior predictive checks assessed the consistency between the maximum likelihood parameter estimation (MLE) and the empirical data.

Extended Mediation Analysis Methods
Hormonal data acquisition. 
Complete information on blood collection, storage and analysis are provided in (Schilliger et al., 2024). Blood was collected in a neutral tube within seven days before or after the MRI, in the morning after an overnight fast. Steroids, including testosterone, estradiol, cortisol and 11-deoxycortisol were measured in the serum by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) in the laboratory of Clinical Chemistry of the Lausanne University Hospital.
Extended Mediation Analysis Methods. Hormonal Mediators. Four pubertal hormones were tested as mediators: DHEA-S (dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate): Adrenal androgen marking adrenarche; neuroprotective and stress-modulating properties. Cortisol: HPA axis glucocorticoid; stress response and pubertal development. Testosterone: Androgenic hormone; stress reactivity, mood, neurodevelopment. Estradiol: Estrogenic hormone; neurotrophic and mood-regulating effects.

Rationale for Hybrid Mediation Approach. Why not mixed-effects mediation models?
Mixed-effects models with CAP × time interactions estimate conditional indirect effects:
(?E[Y|X,M,"time" =0])/?X
These pool information across visits and return marginal effects at a covariate-set baseline, not the observed effect in the actual baseline sample. Our research question targets the absolute (unconditional) mediation effect at each visit:
"ACME"(V_1)=E[Y(M(X=1))-Y(M(X=0))?"visit"=V_1]

This represents the average mediated difference among participants at that specific visit (Imai et al., 2013).
Hybrid solution:
1. Independent models at each timepoint yield visit-specific ACMEs (descriptive statistics)
2. Cluster bootstrap on paired changes (?ACME) provides valid longitudinal inference
3. Maximizes power: all data for cross-sectional estimates; paired data (89% retention) for change tests

Effect Size Measures
* ACME (Average Causal Mediation Effect): Unstandardized indirect effect in original units
* CSIE (Completely Standardized Indirect Effect): All variables (X, M, Y) standardized; facilitates cross-mediator and cross-outcome comparisons

Robust Standard Errors
Given sample size (n < 70 per group) and heteroscedasticity detected in diagnostic analyses, we used HC2 heteroscedasticity-consistent standard errors (sandwich estimator). It provides optimal balance between efficiency, robustness in the presence of heteroscedasticity, and valid inference when residual variance is non-constant.
Sensitivity Analysis Protocol
Unmeasured confounding was assessed by systematically varying rho (residual correlation between mediator and outcome models):
* Range: rho ? [?1, +1] in 0.05 increments
* Baseline: rho_original = 0 (no unmeasured confounding)
* Critical value: rho_critical where 95% CI for indirect effect crosses zero
* Interpretation: Larger |rho_critical| indicates greater robustness to unmeasured confounding
Model Diagnostics
For all regression models underlying mediation:
* Linearity: Residual vs. fitted plots
* Normality: Shapiro-Wilk tests
* Homoscedasticity: Breusch-Pagan tests
* Multicollinearity: Variance Inflation Factors (VIF)
* Influential observations: Cook's distance
