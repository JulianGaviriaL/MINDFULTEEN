Abstract
Mindfulness-based interventions (MBI) decrease psychological distress in adolescents and might prevent the occurrence of affective disorders during this highly vulnerable period. Past research has advanced the discovery of neural architecture recruited by MBI. However, the brain mechanisms through which mindfulness exerts more resilient responses to social stressors in teens remain unclear. Here, we examined how MBI modulates changes in brain network dynamics following social stress with different affective valence (i.e., neutral, negative, and positive). For this aim, we conducted a longitudinal randomized controlled trial in which community-based adolescents (n=63; ages 13–15; MBI: n=30, control: n=33) underwent MBI for 8 weeks. They completed a psychosocial stress task before and following MBI. Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) and self-reported measurements of psychological distress were collected at both measurement points (i.e., pre and post MBI). We computed co-activation patterns (CAPs) on fMRI data to characterize dynamic functional connectivity within whole-brain networks during the recovery phase after social feedback. MBI reduced transient co-activation in dorsal-medial default network (DN) regions following psychosocial stress. However, these changes were not specific to the feedback’s affective valence. Associations between dorsal-medial DN dynamics and symptoms of depression and trait anxiety changed significantly after MBI. Causal mediation analysis revealed that mindfulness trait explained this brain–symptom coupling: post-intervention, the brain–mindfulness trait relationship was selectively weakened, accompanied by a reduction in overall brain–symptom coupling. The mindfulness trait–symptom link remained stable. These effects were robust to unmeasured confounding and not attributable to pubertal maturation. Our findings support a model in which MBI decouples brain–symptom associations related to psychosocial stress in adolescents.


1. Introduction
The present study investigated the impact of mindfulness-based intervention on brain functional systems underlying psychosocial stress response in adolescence, a critical period for the onset of mental disorders, with one in five adolescents affected in Western societies (1,2). This vulnerability stems partly from the exposure of the developing brain to psychosocial stress, compounded by widespread delays in treatment (3,4). Mindfulness-based interventions (MBI) offer a low-cost, scalable approach to reducing stress in youth (5–7). Beyond being a trainable skill, mindfulness is also conceptualized as a trait (8,9) that improves with sustained practice (10). In adolescents, higher mindfulness trait has been associated with reduced anxiety, depression, and somatic symptoms, as well as improved self-esteem and sleep quality (11). However, the brain mechanisms through which mindfulness exerts these effects remain poorly understood (12).

Previous studies have used static fMRI-based functional connectivity (FC) — which estimates time-averaged correlations between brain regions over the entire scan — to identify neural correlates of mindfulness trait, reporting positive associations with default network (DN) regions such as the inferior frontal and orbitofrontal areas (13) and negative associations with the thalamus (13) and precuneus (14). More recently, two studies used dynamic FC (dFC), which tracks changes in functional brain patterns over time (15,16) — to link dFC variability with mindfulness trait: Marusak et al. (17) found decreased dFC in the salience (SN) and central executive (CEN) networks in children and adolescents, and Teng et al (18) reported reduced dFC duration in SN, CEN, and DN areas associated with greater mindfulness trait and attenuated stress responses in young adults. Despite these advances, two questions remain unanswered: how brain dynamics and their coupling with clinical symptoms respond to mindfulness training, and whether these responses differ across types of psychosocial stress. To address these questions, we conducted Mindfulteen (19), a randomized controlled trial (RCT) in which community-recruited adolescents underwent fMRI during acute psychosocial stress, experimentally induced via a modified Montreal Imaging Stress Task [MIST (19,20)],  involving neutral, negative, and positive feedback conditions, both before and after a mindfulness intervention. We analyzed resting-state brain dFC patterns during post-stress recovery to capture lingering stress signatures elicited by the MIST feedback. This approach has revealed finer-grained affective processing signatures in healthy populations (21,22) and in mood disorders (23,24). 

We tested two complementary hypotheses: (i) mindfulness intervention induces changes in brain dFC patterns and modifies their coupling with symptoms of anxiety and depression; and (ii) these effects differ across psychosocial feedback conditions (neutral, negative, positive). In line with prior research, we expected MBI-related changes in distributed circuits, including DN, SN, and CEN (25). Critically, beyond assessing direct brain–symptom associations, we used causal mediation analysis (26) to test and explain how the mindfulness trait mediates this coupling. Unlike conventional mediation, this framework enables evaluation of the assumptions required for causal inference and quantification of robustness to unmeasured confounding (27), including pubertal maturation, which may independently affect brain–symptom relationships.

2. Methods
2.1. Participants. Seventy French-speaking adolescents (ages 13-15) were recruited via social media, hospital consultations, pediatricians, schools, and a dedicated website. Inclusion criteria: no meditation experience, no chronic somatic/psychiatric illness (except mood disorders resolved ?6 months or current anxiety without comorbidities), no psychological treatment, and no MRI contraindications. Most had no psychiatric diagnosis; thirteen reported past depressive episodes or current anxiety on K-SADS-PL (28). No treatment or co-interventions were allowed (Table 1). The final sample comprised 63 adolescents with complete neuroimaging data at both timepoints (MBI: n=30; CTR: n=33; Figure 1).

2.2. Paradigm design. Mindfulteen is a longitudinal randomized controlled trial (19). Participants were randomized via the sealedenvelope® platform to mindfulness?based intervention (MBI) or control (CTR) groups (Figure 1). At baseline (V1), all completed clinical screening and fMRI during the adapted Montreal Imaging Stress Task (MIST; Figure 2A). The MBI group then received 8 weeks of mindfulness training, while controls remained on a waiting list. At follow?up (V2), both groups repeated the same assessments (Figure 2A).

2.3. Task and measures. The fMRI task was an age-adapted MIST with acute psychosocial stressors, social evaluative feedback, and recovery periods (24,25; see Supplementary Methods). Psychiatric assessment used K-SADS-PL (31). Self-report measures included: State-Trait Anxiety Inventory for Children (STAI-C; 32), Beck Depression Inventory (BDI; 33), Depression Anxiety Stress Scale (DASS-21; 34), Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (35), and Child and Adolescent Mindfulness Measure (CAMM; 36). Hormonal measures (DHEA-S, cortisol, testosterone, estradiol) were collected to control for pubertal confounding in mediation analyses (Section 2.6.4.2). See Supplementary Methods for hormonal data acquisition and processing details.

2.4. MRI data acquisition. Resting-state fMRI was acquired on a 3T Siemens Magnetom TIM Trio scanner at the Brain and Behavior Laboratory, University of Geneva. Acquisition and preprocessing details are in Supplementary Methods. 

2.5. Dynamic brain functional connectivity (dFC). We computed seed-based co-activation patterns (CAPs) to capture time-varying functional brain networks from resting-state fMRI (see (32, 33) for full methodology). Seed regions of interest (ROIs; Figure 2B) included mid-frontal gyrus (MFG; MNI: -42,34,20/44,36,20), inferior frontal gyrus (IFG; MNI: 32,26,-14/38,22,-10), and posterior cingulate cortex (PCC; MNI: 1,-55,17). These ROIs were chosen because they were previously associated with stress in adolescents (25), and because they constitute key hubs of default mode (DN), salience (SN), and central executive (CEN) networks previously associated with stress, anxiety, and depression processing (see meta-analytic ROI assessment, Table 2).

2.5.1. Spatiotemporal CAPs metrics. Each CAP comprises a spatial brain map and its temporal dynamics quantified by "entry rates"—the frequency of entering each co-activation state (Figure 2B). Higher entry rates indicate more pattern transitions (i.e., variability); lower entry rates indicate less pattern variability. We assessed CAP entry rates across groups and timepoints, examining between-group differences and within-group changes following 8 weeks of mindfulness training (Figure 2A).

2.6. Statistical analysis.
Linear mixed models (section 2.6.1) identified brain CAPs whose temporal expression (i.e., entry rates) changed in response to the mindfulness intervention (Hypothesis I) and the type of feedback following the MIST task (Hypothesis II). Nonparametric Spearman correlations and Steiger's Z-tests (section 2.6.2) assessed the associations—and their changes over time—between brain CAPs and symptoms as a function of the mindfulness intervention (Hypothesis I) and feedback type (Hypothesis II). Mediation models tested whether mindfulness trait (CAMM) mediated brain CAP–symptom coupling and whether this mediation effect differed between V2 and V1 (section 2.6.3). Two complementary robustness assessments (section 2.6.4, Sensitivity analyses and hormonal mediation) were conducted to disentangle MBI-specific effects from potential confounds (e.g., maturation).

2.6.1. Changes in brain CAPs induced by mindfulness intervention. Linear mixed models (LMM: outcome (CAP) ~ VISIT × GROUP + (1|SUBJECT)) tested whether CAP changes from V1 to V2 differed between MBI and CTR groups. The VISIT × GROUP interaction isolated specific effects of the mindfulness intervention by comparing trajectories and controlling for temporal confounds (34). Random intercepts (1|SUBJECT) accounted for within-subject variance. Parametric bootstraps (5,000 iterations) provided robust 95% CIs, standardized effect sizes emphasized magnitude, and FDR correction addressed multiple comparisons.

2.6.2. Brain–symptom associations. To examine whether mindfulness intervention altered brain–symptom coupling, we computed Spearman's rank correlations between CAP entry rates and four non-redundant clinical measures — STAI-S (state anxiety), STAI-T (trait anxiety), BDI (depression), and DASS-D (stress) — at baseline (V1) and post-intervention (V2). Analyses were restricted to CAPs demonstrating significant VISIT × GROUP interactions in LMM analyses (section 2.6.1), justified by: (1) limited sample size necessitating targeted hypothesis testing, and (2) the rationale that brain CAPs unaffected by MBI are unlikely to show intervention-driven symptom associations; any observed coupling would reflect pre-existing relationships rather than mindfulness effects. To test whether correlation strengths changed between V1 and V2, we applied Steiger's Z-test, which accounts for autocorrelation in dependent correlation comparisons. Robustness was assessed via non-parametric permutation testing (5,000 iterations), in which timepoint labels were randomly shuffled to generate null distributions of correlation differences. All p-values were adjusted using false discovery rate (FDR) correction for multiple testing across CAPs and clinical measures. 

2.6.3. Mindfulness trait mediation of brain–symptom coupling. 
2.6.3.1. Causal mediation models. We tested whether mindfulness trait (CAMM) mediated the relationship between brain CAPs and clinical symptoms with significant V1?V2 changes (Section 2.6.2). We modeled CAP entry rates as the predictor (X), CAMM as the mediator (M), and symptoms as the outcome (Y), following the study's inferential sequence: having identified CAP dynamics altered by the mindfulness intervention (Section 2.6.1) and their symptom coupling (Section 2.6.2), mediation analyses examined which mechanism explains this coupling. In addition, this approach enabled the comparison of alternative mediators (e.g., mindfulness trait vs. pubertal hormones) within the same framework. Causal mediation models included Path a (CAP ? CAMM), Path b (CAMM ? symptoms, controlling for CAP), and Path c? (CAP ? symptoms, controlling for CAMM) at both timepoints. The indirect effect (path a × path b) quantified mediation. 2.6.3.2. Causality in mediation models. Three conditions support causal mediation claims (27). Each was addressed by our design. First, randomized treatment assignment (RCT) ensured that the treatment–mediator and treatment–outcome paths were unconfounded. Second, we assessed robustness through sensitivity analyses quantifying the unmeasured confounders required to overturn mediation estimates, and through alternative mediation models that replaced CAMM with pubertal hormone markers to rule out maturation as a competing pathway. Third, rather than forcing identical path coefficients across MBI and CTR groups — which would assume the intervention affects all paths equally — we estimated separate models for each group, allowing mediation pathways to vary naturally between conditions.

2.6.3.3. Statistical approach for dependent data. Because measures were repeated within subjects, we used a two?step strategy for the mediation models: I) Cross?sectional mediation at each timepoint. Separate models for each group × visit (MBI(V1), MBI(V2), CTR(V1), CTR(V2)) were estimated to obtain indirect effects (ACME) without pooling across visits. II) Longitudinal change with cluster bootstrap. Within?subject changes (?ACME = ACME(V2) – ACME(V1)) were tested using paired?subjects cluster bootstrap (5,000 iterations, stratified by participant ID) to account for within?subject correlation. Bootstrap 95% CIs and two?sided p?values supported inference on intervention?related changes. This design preserves visit?specific estimates while using cluster bootstrap for paired longitudinal inference (35). We also reported completely standardized indirect effects (CSIE) and used HC2 robust standard errors to correct for heteroscedasticity (36).

2.6.4. Control assessments. 2.6.4.1. Sensitivity analyses. We tested robustness to unmeasured confounding by varying the mediator–outcome residual correlation (RHO) from ?1 to +1; larger |RHO critical| values indicate greater robustness (26,37). See Supplementary Methods: Sensitivity Analysis Protocol. Analyses used R packages mediation, boot, sandwich, lmtest, and car. Full diagnostics and hormonal sampling procedures are in Supplementary Methods. 2.6.4.2. Pubertal confounding control. We repeated the mediation using hormonal proxies of puberty (DHEA?S, cortisol, testosterone, estradiol) with the same pathway structure. This tested whether brain–symptom links reflected maturation rather than MBI effects.

3. Results
3.1. Behavioral and Hormonal measures. The LMM-ANOVA analyses of the time (V1, V2) × groups (CTR, MBI) interaction revealed nonsignificant differences in measures of stress, depression, and anxiety, as well as in hormonal markers across visits. Results are described in Table S1. 

3.2. Dynamic functional connectivity (dFC). We computed brain functional CAPs using the mid-frontal gyrus (MFG), inferior frontal gyrus (IFG), and posterior cingulate cortex (PCC) as seed regions (Figure 2B, Table 2). The consensus algorithm identified K=5 as optimal for all seeds (Figure 2C), with high intercluster similarity (mean=78%) across solutions (Figure 2D). Regarding hypothesis I, LMM-ANOVA interactions between groups (MBI vs. CTR)  and visits (V1 vs. V2) were significant in PCC-derived CAPs and nonsignificant for MFG and IFG CAPs. See description of the spatiotemporal expression of these CAPs below. Regarding hypothesis II, LMM-based three-way interactions (GROUP × VISIT × CONDITION) revealed nonsignificant interactions in the MFG, IFG, and PCC CAPs, indicating no differential effects across MIST feedback conditions (neutral, negative, positive; Table S2).
 
3.2.1. Spatial Configuration of PCC-Derived CAPs. Five CAPs were identified from the PCC seed region (Figure 2A), each overlapping with distinct large-scale networks: CAP1DN-DM with the dorsal-medial default network (precuneus, angular gyrus, frontal medial orbital cortex); CAP2DN-MT with the medial temporal default network (hippocampus, parahippocampal gyrus, temporal pole, fusiform gyrus, cerebellum); CAP3FPN with the frontoparietal network (precuneus, cerebellum, angular gyrus, temporal and parietal cortices, frontal mid cortex); CAP4VIS with visual system regions (cuneus, occipital cortex); and CAP5SN with salience network regions (supramarginal gyrus, midcingulate cortex, thalamus). Full descriptions are provided in Tables S3–S7.
 
3.2.2. Temporal Expression of PCC-CAPs (Entry Rates). Group (MBI vs. CTR) × Visit (V1, V2) interactions were significant for CAP1DN-DM (p = 0.03, ?p² = 0.07) and marginal for CAP2DN-MT (p = 0.06, ?p² = 0.06) and CAP3FPN (p = 0.07, ?p² = 0.05). Changes in entry rates for CAP4VIS and CAP5SN were nonsignificant (Table S1). Within-group comparisons. For CAP1DN-DM, entry rates decreased post-intervention in the MBI group (V1?V2: ? = ?0.22, pFDR= 0.07; Figure 3B), whereas controls showed a nonsignificant increase (? = 0.17, pFDR= 0.17; Figure 3C). For CAP2DN-MT and CAP3FPN, entry rates increased from V1 to V2 in controls (CAP2: ? = 0.23, pFDR= 0.05; CAP3: ? = 0.37, pFDR= 0.01; Figure 3C) but showed nonsignificant changes in the MBI group (CAP2: ? = ?0.15, pFDR= 0.25; CAP3: ? = ?0.04, pFDR = 0.76; Figure 3B). Model fit was adequate (Figure S3).

3.3. Associations Between Brain PCC-CAPs and Clinical Symptoms. When MIST conditions were aggregated (testing Hypothesis I), CAP1DN-DM entries showed marginal correlations with depression (BDI) and trait anxiety (STAI-T) at baseline (V1; Figure 4A). Critically, these correlations weakened significantly from V1 to V2 in the MBI group only (BDI: ?RHO = ?0.46, Steiger's Z = ?2.59, p = .02; STAI-T: ?RHO = ?0.50, Steiger's Z = ?2.21, p = .03. Figure 4B, Table S8), with no significant changes in controls (all p > .05; Figure 4B; ). When MIST feedback conditions (neutral, negative, positive) were analyzed separately (i.e., testing Hypothesis II), no significant associations between CAP entry rates and clinical symptoms were observed (Figure S4).

3.4. Causal Mediation Analysis
The mindfulness trait mediates CAP1DN-DM–symptom coupling. Having established that CAP1DN-DM–symptom correlations weakened significantly from V1 to V2 in the MBI group (Section 3.3), we examined whether the mindfulness trait (CAMM) accounted for this decoupling and whether its mediation (i.e., explanatory role) changed over time.
 
3.4.1. Cross-sectional Mindfulness Trait Mediation. At baseline (V1), CAMM significantly mediated the CAP1DN-DM–symptom relationship in the MBI group for both depression and trait anxiety (BDI: ACME = 0.25, p = .04; STAI-T: ACME = 0.23, p = .04; Table 3). This mediation was driven by a significant negative Path a (CAP1DN-DM? CAMM: ? = ?0.35, p = .04), combined with a significant negative Path b (CAMM ? symptoms: BDI: ? = ?0.72, p < .01; STAI-T: ? = ?0.66, p < .01; Figure 5A–B). Direct effects were nonsignificant for both outcomes (Table S11). For depression, the total effect was also nonsignificant, consistent with full mediation; for trait anxiety, the total effect was significant while the direct was not, indicating partial mediation (Table 3). At post-intervention (V2), the mediation was no longer significant (BDI: ACME = ?0.07, p = .64; STAI-T: ACME = ?0.09, p = .64). Path a reversed direction and became nonsignificant (? = 0.11, p = .65), while Path b remained significant (BDI: ? = ?0.59, p < .01; STAI-T: ? = ?0.77, p < .01; Figure 5A–B). The CTR group showed nonsignificant mediation at both timepoints (Tables 3 and S10; Figure 5).

3.4.2. Longitudinal Changes in Mindfulness Trait Mediation. To assess changes in mediation over time, we computed differences in ACME between visits (?ACME = ACME(V2) – ACME(V1)) using a paired-subjects bootstrap. In the MBI group, ACME decreased significantly for both outcomes (BDI: ?ACME = ?0.30, p = .01, 95% CI [?0.50, ?0.05]; STAI-T: ?ACME = ?0.32, p = .03, 95% CI [?0.52, ?0.02]. Table 3), confirming weakened CAP1DN-DM? CAMM ? symptom mediation post-intervention. This change was driven by Path a (CAP1DN-DM? CAMM), which reversed from negative to positive coupling (?a = 0.48, p = .01 for both outcomes), while Path b (CAMM ? symptoms) remained stable (BDI: ?b = 0.10, p = .46; STAI-T: ?b = ?0.14, p = .15). Total effects decreased significantly for STAI-T (?c = ?0.63, p < .001) and marginally for BDI (?c = ?0.35, p = .08). Direct effects decreased significantly for STAI-T (?c? = ?0.32, p = .004) but not for BDI (?c? = ?0.05, p = .70; Table S11). In controls, mediation changes were nonsignificant (BDI: ?ACME = 0.05, p = .38; STAI-T: ?ACME = 0.06, p = .26), though total and direct effects increased significantly for STAI-T (?c = 0.42, p = .01; ?c? = 0.36, p = .03). 

3.4.3. Control assessments. 
3.4.3.1. Sensitivity analyses.  Sensitivity analyses indicated high robustness: significant ACME estimates at V1 would remain significant unless an unmeasured confounder correlated with CAMM–symptom residuals at |RHO| ? 0.80 (Table S12). 3.4.3.2. Pubertal confounding control. None of the hormonal markers of puberty (testosterone, estradiol, DHEA, cortisol) mediated the association between CAP1DN-DM  and clinical symptoms (BDI, STAI-T). Average causal mediation effects (ACME) were nonsignificant at both timepoints, and longitudinal changes (? ACME) showed no significant variation from V1 to V2. Quantitative results are detailed in Tables S14-S17.

3.5. Summary of Results.
Hypothesis I (aggregated MIST conditions) was supported by three converging findings: First, linear mixed models revealed that the mindfulness intervention selectively reduced the temporal expression of CAP1DN-DM in the MBI group, whereas controls showed the opposite pattern in CAP2DN-MT and CAP3FPN (Section 3.2). Second, Spearman correlations between CAP1DN-DM and clinical symptoms (BDI, STAI-T) significantly weakened from V1 to V2 in the MBI group only (Steiger's Z tests; Section 3.3). Third, causal mediation analysis demonstrated that mindfulness trait (CAMM) mediated the CAP1DN-DM–symptom coupling: significant longitudinal reductions in the indirect pathway (CAP1DN-DM ? CAMM ? symptoms) occurred exclusively in the MBI group, driven by a reversal of the brain–mindfulness coupling (Path a; Section 3.4.2). These mediation effects were robust to unmeasured confounding (sensitivity threshold |RHO| ? 0.80), and pubertal hormonal markers showed null mediation, ruling out alternative maturational pathways (Section 3.4.3). Hypothesis II (MIST condition-specific effects) was not supported: neither CAP temporal expression, nor brain–symptom associations differed across feedback conditions (neutral, negative, positive; Sections 3.2 and 3.3).

4. Discussion
We evaluated the impact of mindfulness on the neural response to psychosocial stressors in community-based adolescents. Specifically, we tested whether (i) the temporal dynamics of functional brain activity (i.e., CAP entry rates) and their brain–symptom coupling change in response to mindfulness intervention; and (ii) whether these dynamics and their symptom coupling differ across psychosocial feedback conditions. Our results confirmed the first hypothesis, as discussed next, whereas changes in feedback valence (neutral, negative, positive) had no significant effects.

4.1. Brain CAPs linked to mindfulness intervention. In line with Hypothesis I, we observed changes in brain dFC in response to mindfulness training. The co-activation pattern most responsive to MBI encompassed dorsal-medial areas of the DN (CAP1DN-DM). In the MBI group, CAP1DN-DM entry rates decreased from V1 to V2, whereas controls showed the opposite pattern in CAP2DN-MT and CAP3FPN (Section 3.2). These findings are consistent with reports of DN hyperconnectivity in adolescents experiencing acute stress (25), posttraumatic stress symptoms (38), and depression (39,40), and suggest that MBI may counteract stress-related DN recruitment at the brain level.

4.2. Brain CAPs and clinical symptoms. At baseline, greater CAP1DN-DM entry rates were associated with higher depression and trait anxiety in the MBI group (Figure S5), and these correlations weakened significantly from V1 to V2 in the MBI group only (Figure 4A). Extensive research links enhanced DN activity to acute stress, anxiety, and depression (41–44). However, correlation does not establish a mechanism. We therefore used causal mediation analysis to test whether mindfulness trait causally mediates (i.e., explains) this brain–behavior coupling.

4.3. Mindfulness trait causally mediates brain–symptom coupling. The causal mediation framework decomposed the brain–symptom relationship into an indirect effect (ACME: CAP1DN-DM ? CAMM ? symptoms) and a direct effect (ACDE: CAP1DN-DM ? symptoms). When the indirect path is significant but the direct is not, the brain–symptom association is explained by mindfulness trait rather than by an independent mechanism. At baseline, this was the case for both depression and trait anxiety: greater CAP1DN-DM entry rates were associated with lower mindfulness trait (Path a; Figure 5), which in turn was associated with higher symptoms (Path b; Figure 5). For depression, the total effect was also nonsignificant, consistent with full mediation; for trait anxiety, the total effect was significant while the direct was not, indicating partial mediation (Section 3.4.1; Table 3).

Critically, the intervention disrupted this mediation. At post-MBI (V2), the indirect pathway was no longer significant — driven selectively by Path a, which reversed direction, while Path b remained stable. The mindfulness intervention thus decoupled brain dynamics from mindfulness trait, rather than altering the mindfulness–symptom relationship. Longitudinal analyses confirmed significant reductions in the indirect pathway in the MBI group only; total effects also decreased significantly for trait anxiety and marginally for depression (Section 3.4.2; Figure 5C). Conversely, controls showed no significant mediation changes (?ACME), but total and direct effects increased significantly for trait anxiety — indicating that CAP1DN-DM–symptom coupling in this group was not explained by mindfulness trait (Figure 5C). These mediation effects were robust to unmeasured confounding and not attributable to pubertal maturation (Section 4.4). This model aligns with findings linking acute stress to mind wandering (45) and DN hyperconnectivity (46), and extends them by explaining how mindfulness intervention disrupts this brain–symptom coupling to reduce psychological distress.

4.4. Robustness and causality. The present study satisfied the conditions to support causality in the mediation models (27). First, randomized treatment assignment ensured that the treatment–mediator and treatment–outcome paths were unconfounded. Second, two robustness assessments confirmed resilience to unmeasured confounding: sensitivity analyses indicated that mediation estimates would remain significant unless an unmeasured confounder produced very high residual correlations (Table S12), supporting the sequential ignorability assumption, and hormonal mediation using pubertal markers yielded null indirect effects, ruling out maturation as an alternative pathway (Section 3.4.3). Third, group-specific mediation models allowed all path coefficients to vary freely across MBI and CTR conditions, avoiding constrained interaction assumptions.

5. Limitations and Future Directions.
The sample size precluded the investigation of sex-related differences in dFC changes elicited by the mindfulness intervention. Our CAP analyses could not differentiate neural responses to the affective valence of psychosocial stressors (Hypothesis II); alternative dFC markers may be needed to capture condition-specific effects. The study lacked long-term follow-up; whether the observed changes persist beyond the immediate post-intervention period remains unknown. Future research should replicate these findings in larger and more diverse samples, incorporate multimodal data including neuroendocrine measures, implement long-term follow-up across developmental stages, and explore whether baseline individual differences moderate the intervention's neural effects.

6. Conclusions
Using dFC co-activation patterns from an RCT, we found that mindfulness training selectively altered the temporal expression of CAP1DN-DM (significant group × time interaction) and weakened the associations between CAP1DN-DM and symptoms of depression and trait anxiety. Causal mediation analysis revealed that this brain–symptom decoupling was mediated by mindfulness trait: at baseline, CAP1DN-DM influenced symptoms via its negative association with mindfulness trait, and the intervention selectively disrupted this brain–mindfulness trait coupling. These effects were absent in controls, robust to unmeasured confounding, and not attributable to pubertal maturation. We propose a brain–behavior model in which the mindfulness intervention attenuates dorsal-medial DN recruitment and reduces psychological distress in adolescents through a causal pathway involving mindfulness trait. The present study highlights a novel dFC metric of mindfulness-mediated brain–symptom coupling associated with a brain state overlapping with networks implicated in self-reflective processing, memory, and affective regulation, with a key node in the PCC. Other CAPs defined by their dFC with prefrontal cortical nodes did not show such modulation.
